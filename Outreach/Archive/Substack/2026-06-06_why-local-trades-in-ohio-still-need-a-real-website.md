# Why local trades in Ohio still need a real website

I'm out talking to lawn care companies, HVAC techs, plumbers, auto repair shops, and handymen across Circleville, Chillicothe, and the counties around them. Most of these businesses do solid work. They have the reviews, the repeat customers, and the trucks people recognize on the road.

A lot of them are still losing jobs they should get because of how they show up — or don't — online.

## The dead link on the listings

One lawn care company serving the Circleville and Amanda area has 11 years in business, an A+ BBB rating, and strong Google reviews. Their Facebook page shows real jobs and equipment.

Google, MapQuest, and directories all point to their old domain. Click it from a phone and it goes nowhere. The homeowner who saw the truck or got a neighbor's recommendation searches the name later, hits a dead link, and calls the next company in the results instead.

That one is easy to spot once you look. It is not rare.

## "We just use Facebook"

Plenty of small auto shops and service businesses operate this way. The Google Business Profile has the phone and hours. Facebook has photos and updates. It works fine for people who already know them or see the work in person.

It falls short for the person searching "AC repair Chillicothe" at 7pm on a hot day or "mowing service near me" after driving past a clean yard. Those searches need a destination that feels like the actual business — fast, clear, and controlled by the owner.

## The site that checks the box

Some have a website. It was probably built years ago or through one of the low-cost outfits. It has the services listed and a contact form. On a phone — where most of these searches happen — it requires zooming and scrolling. Updating a phone number or adding a new offering means chasing down the original person who built it.

It exists, so they feel like the digital part is handled. In practice, it does not make it easy for a customer to pick them over a competitor who shows up looking current and reachable.

## What actually helps

For a local trade business, a useful website does not need stock photos of people who don't work there, heavy animations, or a dozen pages. It needs to load fast on a phone, even with spotty signal. The phone number should be big and tap-to-call. Services and the actual service area should be obvious. A simple form lets someone request a quote after hours without playing phone tag.

That is the core of the Essential tier: clean, mobile-first, professional without the extras. Most of those projects land between $1,650 and $1,750 all-in. One time. You own the domain and the site. Ongoing support is optional if you want help with security, small updates, or local visibility later.

## It won't create work that isn't there

If the quality or reliability is not there, a website will not fix it. The owners I talk to already have the hard part handled. A straightforward site just stops leaving easy leads on the table when someone searches or follows up on a recommendation.

## Check it this week

Pull up your phone. Search your business name plus your town the way a new customer would. Click the website link in your Google listing if there is one. See what actually shows up.

If it does not match the quality of the work you do every day, that is the gap.
